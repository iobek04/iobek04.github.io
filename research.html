Sunk Costs and Persistence

Computation, Learning, and Development Lab

Web-Surf Task

When do people cut their losses? In the Web-Surf Task, people move through video galleries with a limited amount of time. Each offer tells them how many seconds a video will take to load, and they decide whether to wait for it or skip to the next gallery.

Because waiting costs time that can't be recovered, the task lets us study how time already invested shapes the decision to persist versus abandon, and how sensitivity to sunk costs differs across people.

The Development of Goal Commitment

In collaboration with: Eleanor Holton and Dr. Kate Nussenbaum

Submarine Fishing Task

Pursuing a goal means balancing persistence with the flexibility to abandon it when a better alternative comes along. Adults often over-persist after investing in a goal, even when switching is clearly better. We ask how goal pursuit strategies and susceptibility to sunk costs change across development.

The task is taken from Holton et al. (2024) [1]. On each trial, people choose among three sea creatures to fill as many nets as they can. Nets fill up gradually but can only hold one type of creature, so switching forfeits the progress made so far. How much each creature offers drifts over time, with occasional larger jumps.

Learning Progress and Exploration

Computation, Learning, and Development Lab

Garden Search Task

How do people decide where to explore next, and how do they keep track of how much they are learning along the way? We are building a child-friendly search game based on the spatially correlated grid task from Schulz et al. (2019) [2]: players help characters like Sprout, Drizzle and Poppy search a garden for points, with only a limited number of clicks per garden.

We're still finalizing this task, so there's no demo yet.

[2] Schulz, E., Wu, C. M., Ruggeri, A., & Meder, B. (2019). Searching for rewards like a child means less generalization and more directed exploration. Psychological Science, 30(11), 1561–1572.

Inferring Learning Progress in Humans and Machines

Honors thesis with: Annya Dahmani, Dr. Alison Gopnik and Dr. Sharon Thompson-Schill

Leaper Task

How do people infer how much someone has learned? For my honors thesis, we used a Leaper game, in which a player crosses a river by hopping across logs, to understand how people infer learning progress. Participants saw which level a player started on and which level the player chose next, then judged how much of the first level the player had completed.

We compared human participants with large language models in goal-oriented versus open-ended environments to see how they respond differently. This work builds on Dahmani et al. (2023) [3].
